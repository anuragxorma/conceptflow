## 1. Why Nested Diagrams Exist

### The scaling problem

Start with a many-valued context: 50 Eurovision winners, each described by a handful of
numeric or categorical attributes — song tempo (BPM), key mode (major/minor), and several
voting-pattern scores. Binary FCA requires a binary context, so each many-valued attribute
is passed through a *conceptual scale* that turns it into one or more binary attributes.
The result is a formal context $K$ with 50 objects and 7 binary attributes.

Computing $\mathcal{B}(K)$ and drawing its Hasse diagram is straightforward, but the
diagram can be hard to read when attributes belong to conceptually separate domains.
Seven attributes might all live together in one crowded lattice, obscuring structure
that was obvious before scaling.

The solution: split the 7 attributes into two groups by domain — 4 *outer*
(voting patterns) and 3 *inner* (musical properties) — and analyse them separately.
This gives two factor lattices:

```
K_outer = (G, M_outer, I_outer),    with 16 concepts
K_inner = (G, M_inner, I_inner),    with 10 concepts
```

Together they are far simpler than $\mathcal{B}(K)$ would be. But the price is that we
have lost the relationship between the two domains. The nested diagram recovers it
visually.

### Direct product vs subdirect product

The naïve recovery would be to display every pair of concepts from the two factor
lattices — *all* 16 × 10 = 160 pairs. But most of these pairs do not correspond to any
actual concept of $\mathcal{B}(K)$.

> **Definition — Direct product vs subdirect product**
>
> The **direct product** $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer}) \times \mathcal{B}(K_\text{inner})$
> contains all 160 pairs. The **subdirect product** — the image of the embedding
> $\varphi\colon \mathcal{B}(K) \to \mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer}) \times \mathcal{B}(K_\text{inner})$
> — contains only the pairs that correspond to actual concepts of $\mathcal{B}(K)$.
> Both projections of the subdirect product are surjective, so every outer concept and
> every inner concept appears at least once.

### The nested diagram

A nested line diagram (Ganter & Wille §4.1) encodes the subdirect product as follows:

- Draw the outer lattice $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer})$ as the skeleton.
- At each outer node $c_\text{outer}$, place a copy of the inner lattice $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{inner})$.
- Fill the inner node $c_\text{inner}$ at $c_\text{outer}$ if and only if the pair
  $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$ lies in the subdirect product.
- Leave it hollow otherwise.

A hollow node is not an error — it is padding that preserves the inner lattice's shape
for visual alignment across all outer nodes. The filled nodes at each outer position tell
you exactly which inner concepts co-occur with that outer concept in $\mathcal{B}(K)$.

> **Key insight:** The nested diagram is a visual representation of the subdirect
> embedding $\varphi$. Filled means "in $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$"; hollow means
> "not in $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$".

The central question — and the focus of the rest of this tutorial — is:
**how do you efficiently compute which inner nodes are filled?**

## 2. The Filling Algorithm: Coordinate Pairs and Join-Closure

### The embedding φ, defined

Let $K = K_\text{outer} \mid K_\text{inner}$ denote the *apposition*: the formal context
with the same objects $G$ and attribute set $M_\text{outer} \cup M_\text{inner}$ (the
union is disjoint). For any concept $C = (A, B) \in \mathcal{B}(K)$, define its two
projections:

$$
\pi_\text{outer}(C) = \text{concept of } K_\text{outer} \text{ with extent }
\{g \in G : \forall m \in B \cap M_\text{outer},\; (g,m) \in I\}
$$

$$
\pi_\text{inner}(C) = \text{concept of } K_\text{inner} \text{ with extent }
\{g \in G : \forall m \in B \cap M_\text{inner},\; (g,m) \in I\}
$$

$$
\varphi(C) = \bigl(\pi_\text{outer}(C),\; \pi_\text{inner}(C)\bigr)
$$

$\varphi$ is injective and its image is the subdirect product (Ganter & Wille,
Theorem 4.3).

> **Definition — Filled inner node**
>
> The inner node $c_\text{inner}$ is drawn **filled** at outer node $c_\text{outer}$
> in the nested diagram if and only if
> $$
> (c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner}) \in \mathrm{image}(\varphi)
> $$
> i.e., there exists a concept $C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$ such that
> $\varphi(C) = (c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$.

### The naïve algorithm and its problem

One approach: build $K$, compute $\mathcal{B}(K)$ by closure, map each concept through
$\varphi$, and collect all pairs. This is correct but defeats the purpose of using factor
lattices — the whole context $K$ is exactly what we wanted to avoid working with directly.

### The join-closure algorithm

The efficient algorithm works entirely within the two factor lattices. It requires no
knowledge of $\mathcal{B}(K)$ beyond the object concepts of each factor.

> **Definition — Join-closure algorithm**
>
> **Seed set:**
> $$
> P_0 = \bigl\{(\gamma_\text{outer}(g),\; \gamma_\text{inner}(g)) : g \in G\bigr\}
>       \cup \bigl\{(\bot_\text{outer},\; \bot_\text{inner})\bigr\}
> $$
> where $\gamma_\text{outer}(g)$ is the object concept of $g$ in $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer})$
> and similarly for inner.
>
> **Closure step:** repeat until no new pairs appear:
> $$
> \text{if } (c_o, c_i) \in P \text{ and } (c_o', c_i') \in P,
> \text{ add } (c_o \vee c_o',\; c_i \vee c_i') \text{ to } P
> $$
>
> **Result:** $P^* = \mathrm{image}(\varphi)$.

The join $c \vee c'$ in each factor lattice is the smallest concept whose extent contains
$\mathrm{ext}(c) \cup \mathrm{ext}(c')$. Why this algorithm produces exactly
$\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ — no more and no less — is the content of Part 3.

The bottom pair $(\bot_\text{outer}, \bot_\text{inner})$ is added explicitly because
$\bot_K$ has an empty extent, so no single object maps to it, yet it must be represented.

## 3. Why P\* = image(φ): The Core Theorem

The equality $P^* = \mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ rests on two independent propositions.
Together they give both containments.

### Proposition 1: every concept equals the join of its object concepts

> **Theorem (Proposition 1)**
>
> For any concept $C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$:
> $$
> C = \bigvee\{\gamma(g) : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}
> $$

> *Proof.*
> Write $A = \mathrm{ext}(C)$. For each $g \in A$, the object concept $\gamma(g)$
> is the smallest concept containing $g$. Since $C$ contains $g$, we have $\gamma(g) \leq C$
> for all $g \in A$, so the join $J = \bigvee\{\gamma(g) : g \in A\}$ satisfies $J \leq C$.
>
> For the reverse: $\mathrm{ext}(J) = \bigl(\bigcup\{\mathrm{ext}(\gamma(g)) : g \in A\}\bigr)''$.
> Since $g \in \mathrm{ext}(\gamma(g))$ for every $g$, we have
> $A \subseteq \bigcup\{\mathrm{ext}(\gamma(g)) : g \in A\}$,
> so $A'' \subseteq \mathrm{ext}(J)$. But $A'' = A$ because $A = \mathrm{ext}(C)$
> is $K$-closed. Thus $A \subseteq \mathrm{ext}(J)$, meaning $C \leq J$.
>
> Both inequalities give $C = J$. $\square$

### Proposition 2: φ preserves joins

> **Theorem (Proposition 2)**
>
> For any $C_1, C_2 \in \mathcal{B}(K)$:
> $$
> \varphi(C_1 \vee C_2) = \varphi(C_1) \vee \varphi(C_2)
> $$
> where the right-hand join is taken componentwise in
> $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer}) \times \mathcal{B}(K_\text{inner})$.

> *Proof.*
> In the apposition $K = K_\text{outer} \mid K_\text{inner}$, the closure of a set of
> objects under the outer attributes depends only on their outer incidences. Formally:
> $$
> \mathrm{ext}(\pi_\text{outer}(C_1 \vee C_2))
> = K_\text{outer}\text{-closure of }
>   \bigl(\mathrm{ext}(C_1) \cup \mathrm{ext}(C_2)\bigr)''_K
> $$
> The $K$-closure does not shrink a set below its $K_\text{outer}$-closure, so:
> $$
> K_\text{outer}\text{-closure of }
> \bigl(\mathrm{ext}(C_1) \cup \mathrm{ext}(C_2)\bigr)''_K
> = K_\text{outer}\text{-closure of }
>   \bigl(\mathrm{ext}(C_1) \cup \mathrm{ext}(C_2)\bigr)
> = \mathrm{ext}\bigl(\pi_\text{outer}(C_1) \vee \pi_\text{outer}(C_2)\bigr)
> $$
> Therefore $\pi_\text{outer}(C_1 \vee C_2) = \pi_\text{outer}(C_1) \vee \pi_\text{outer}(C_2)$.
> The argument for the inner projection is symmetric. $\square$

### The main theorem: P\* = image(φ)

> **Main Theorem**
>
> The join-closure $P^*$ of the seed set $P_0$ equals $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$.

> *Proof — two containments.*
>
> **$P^* \subseteq \mathrm{image}(\varphi)$.**
> The seed set $P_0$ consists of pairs $\varphi(\gamma(g))$ and $\varphi(\bot_K)$, all lying
> in $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$. The join-closure adds joins of existing pairs. By
> Proposition 2, any join of pairs in $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ is again in
> $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$. By induction, $P^* \subseteq \mathrm{image}(\varphi)$.
>
> **$\mathrm{image}(\varphi) \subseteq P^*$.**
> Take any $C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$. By Proposition 1,
> $C = \bigvee\{\gamma(g) : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}$. Applying $\varphi$ and
> Proposition 2:
> $$
> \varphi(C)
> = \bigvee\{\varphi(\gamma(g)) : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}
> = \bigvee\{(\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g),\; \gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g))
>            : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}
> \in P^*
> $$
> The second equality uses the bridging lemma (Part 4). If $\mathrm{ext}(C) = \emptyset$
> then $C = \bot_K$ and $\varphi(\bot_K) = (\bot_\text{outer}, \bot_\text{inner})
> \in P_0 \subseteq P^*$. $\square$

## 4. The Bridging Lemma: Why K Is Never Needed

Parts 2 and 3 established that $P^* = \mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ when the seed pairs
are $\{(\pi_\text{outer}(\gamma_K(g)),\; \pi_\text{inner}(\gamma_K(g))) : g \in G\}$.
Computing these seeds naïvely requires knowing the object concepts $\gamma_K(g)$ inside
$\mathcal{B}(K)$, which requires building $K$.

The bridging lemma shortcuts this: it says the projections of $\gamma_K(g)$ equal the
object concepts inside each factor lattice directly.

### The apposition structure

Recall that $K = K_\text{outer} \mid K_\text{inner}$ is the apposition: the same objects
$G$, attribute set $M_\text{outer} \sqcup M_\text{inner}$ (disjoint union), and incidence
defined componentwise:

```
(g, m) ∈ I_K  iff  (g, m) ∈ I_outer   (when m ∈ M_outer)
              iff  (g, m) ∈ I_inner   (when m ∈ M_inner)
```

The disjointness $M_\text{outer} \cap M_\text{inner} = \emptyset$ is essential. The
implementation enforces this with a runtime `ValueError`.

### The lemma

> **Lemma — Bridging Lemma**
>
> In the apposition $K = K_\text{outer} \mid K_\text{inner}$, for every object $g \in G$:
> $$
> \pi_\text{outer}(\gamma_K(g)) = \gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g)
> \qquad
> \pi_\text{inner}(\gamma_K(g)) = \gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g)
> $$
> The projection of the $K$-object concept equals the factor-lattice object concept.

> *Proof (outer projection; inner is symmetric).*
>
> Let $A = \mathrm{ext}(\gamma_K(g)) = \{g\}''_K$. We need to show that the
> $K_\text{outer}$-closure of $A$ equals $\{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$.
>
> **Step 1.** $A \subseteq \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$. Any $h \in A$ shares all of $g$'s
> $K$-attributes, in particular all outer attributes, so $h \in \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$.
>
> **Step 2.** Sandwiching. We have $g \in A$, so $g \in A \subseteq \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$.
> Taking $K_\text{outer}$-closures and using that $\{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$ is already
> closed:
> $$
> \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}
> \subseteq A''_{K_\text{outer}}
> \subseteq \bigl(\{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}\bigr)''_{K_\text{outer}}
> = \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}
> $$
> So $A''_{K_\text{outer}} = \{g\}''_{K_\text{outer}}$, i.e.,
> $\mathrm{ext}(\pi_\text{outer}(\gamma_K(g))) = \mathrm{ext}(\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g))$.
> $\square$

### Consequence

By the bridging lemma, the seed set of the join-closure algorithm becomes:

$$
P_0 = \bigl\{(\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g),\; \gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g)) : g \in G\bigr\}
      \cup \bigl\{(\bot_\text{outer},\; \bot_\text{inner})\bigr\}
$$

Both $\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g)$ and $\gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g)$ are computed directly from
the factor lattices, without ever constructing $K$ or $\mathcal{B}(K)$.

> **Key insight:** The bridging lemma is the keystone. Part 3 proved that
> $\mathrm{image}(\varphi) = $ join-closure of $\{\varphi(\gamma_K(g))\}$. The
> bridging lemma proves that $\varphi(\gamma_K(g)) = (\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g),\,
> \gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g))$, making the whole computation factor-local. The combined
> context $K$ is an abstract object referenced in the proof but never materialised
> in code.

## 5. Implementation Walkthrough

### From many-valued data to factor lattices

`ManyValuedContext` stores the raw data table as a pandas DataFrame. `ConceptualScaler`
transforms it into a binary `FormalContext` by applying `Scale` objects to each
many-valued attribute. Two separate scalers produce the two factor contexts:

```python
# builder.root() internally does:
outer_context = ConceptualScaler(scales=outer_scales, output="context").fit_transform(mvc)
# → FormalContext with objects=winners, attributes=M_outer

# builder.expand_all() internally does, for the top outer concept:
object_names = [mvc.objects[i] for i in sorted(top_concept.extent)]
inner_context = ConceptualScaler(scales=inner_scales, output="context").fit_transform(
    mvc.restrict_objects(object_names)  # full extent for template
)
```

The crucial invariant: both scalers iterate over `mvc.objects` in the same DataFrame row
order. Object index $i$ in $K_\text{outer}$ is the same winner as object index $i$ in
$K_\text{inner}$. The filling algorithm depends on this shared index — an assertion in
the code verifies it before any fills are computed.

### The ExplorationView tree

The data structure is an `ExplorationView` tree. The root holds $K_\text{outer}$ and its
lattice. Each outer concept can have a child `ExplorationView` for its inner context. For
the nested diagram, only the top outer concept gets a child — this child's lattice is the
"inner template" used for all outer nodes.

```python
# root view: K_outer
view.context    # FormalContext K_outer
view.lattice    # ConceptLattice B(K_outer)

# inner template: child of the top outer concept
top_id = find_top(view.lattice)
template_view = view.children[top_id]
template_view.context   # FormalContext K_inner (all objects)
template_view.lattice   # ConceptLattice B(K_inner)
```

### _gamma_local: finding the object concept

```python
def _gamma_local(lattice) -> dict[int, str]:
    """γ(g): stable_id of the concept with the SMALLEST extent still containing g."""
    result = {}
    for obj_idx in range(lattice.context.n_objects):
        candidates = [c for c in lattice.concepts if obj_idx in c.extent]
        if candidates:
            result[obj_idx] = min(candidates, key=lambda c: len(c.extent)).stable_id()
    return result
```

The concept with the smallest extent containing $g$ is exactly $\gamma(g)$ by definition —
the object concept is the most specific concept that still has $g$ in scope. The real
function computes this for every object in the lattice at once, keyed by object index.

### _compute_filled_pairs: the join-closure loop

```python
def _compute_filled_pairs(
    outer_lattice,
    inner_lattice,
    gamma_outer: dict[int, str],   # obj_idx → outer concept stable_id
    gamma_inner: dict[int, str],   # obj_idx → inner concept stable_id
) -> dict[str, set[str]]:
    outer_concepts = list(outer_lattice.concepts)
    inner_concepts = list(inner_lattice.concepts)
    outer_ext = {c.stable_id(): c.extent for c in outer_concepts}
    inner_ext = {c.stable_id(): c.extent for c in inner_concepts}

    def _lat_join(ext_by_id, concept_list, id1, id2):
        # Join = smallest concept whose extent ⊇ extent(id1) ∪ extent(id2).
        combined = ext_by_id[id1] | ext_by_id[id2]
        best = None
        for c in concept_list:
            if combined <= c.extent and (best is None or len(c.extent) < len(best.extent)):
                best = c
        return best.stable_id()

    def outer_join(id1, id2):
        return _lat_join(outer_ext, outer_concepts, id1, id2)

    def inner_join(id1, id2):
        return _lat_join(inner_ext, inner_concepts, id1, id2)

    # Seed: atomic pairs + explicit bottom pair
    pairs = {(gamma_outer[i], gamma_inner[i]) for i in gamma_outer if i in gamma_inner}
    bot_outer = min(outer_concepts, key=lambda c: len(c.extent)).stable_id()
    bot_inner = min(inner_concepts, key=lambda c: len(c.extent)).stable_id()
    pairs.add((bot_outer, bot_inner))

    # Close under componentwise joins until fixpoint
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        pairs_list = list(pairs)
        for i in range(len(pairs_list)):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(pairs_list)):
                o1, i1 = pairs_list[i]
                o2, i2 = pairs_list[j]
                new_pair = (outer_join(o1, o2), inner_join(i1, i2))
                if new_pair not in pairs:
                    pairs.add(new_pair)
                    changed = True

    result: dict[str, set[str]] = {}
    for oid, iid in pairs:
        result.setdefault(oid, set()).add(iid)
    return result
```

This is the real implementation from `src/conceptflow/visualization/nested.py`,
reproduced verbatim (the gamma dicts are computed once by `_gamma_local` and
passed in, rather than recomputed inside this function).

### Why the object ordering matters

The index $i$ is used as a shared key to pair $\gamma_\text{outer}(g)$ with
$\gamma_\text{inner}(g)$ for the same object $g$. If the outer and inner contexts stored
their objects in different orders, index 3 in the outer lattice would be a different
winner than index 3 in the inner lattice, and the seed pairs would be wrong — pairing
unrelated concepts. The assertion catches this before any damage is done.

### The disjointness check

Before the assertion, the code also verifies $M_\text{outer} \cap M_\text{inner} = \emptyset$.
If a scale attribute name appears in both factor contexts, the apposition structure breaks
and the bridging lemma's sandwiching argument fails. A runtime `ValueError` with the
overlapping names is raised.

## 6. Worked Example: Nine Eurovision Winners

We trace the join-closure algorithm on a 9-winner subset of the Eurovision dataset. All
values are from the actual implementation output. The full 50-winner context is built by
`examples/eurovision_nested_diagram.py`.

### Attribute notation

| Symbol | Full attribute     | Scale type              |
|--------|--------------------|-------------------------|
| **p**  | political_support  | outer (GeneralScale)    |
| **r**  | regional_support   | outer (GeneralScale)    |
| **c**  | cultural_support   | outer (GeneralScale)    |
| **h**  | historical_support | outer (GeneralScale)    |
| **a**  | bpm ≥ 100          | inner (ThresholdScale)  |
| **b**  | bpm ≥ 150          | inner (ThresholdScale)  |
| **m**  | minor key          | inner (DichotomicScale) |

A concept is identified by its intent. The **top** concept has intent $\emptyset$
(no constraints, all objects); the **bottom** has the most specific intent.

#### A key computational fact

In any concept lattice, the join of two concepts in terms of their intents is the
*intersection*:

$$
\mathrm{int}(C_1 \vee C_2) = \mathrm{int}(C_1) \cap \mathrm{int}(C_2)
$$

Larger intents (more constraints) correspond to smaller concepts lower in the lattice.
Joining two concepts moves upward, keeping only the shared constraints.

|------|-------------------|---------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| 1982 | Nicole            | DE      | `{p, h}`                  | `{a, m}`                  |
| 1992 | Linda Martin      | IE      | `{r, c, h}`               | `∅` (top)                 |
| 1999 | Charlotte Nilsson | SE      | `{p, r, c}`               | `{a}`                     |

We join each pair of seeds using $\mathrm{int}(C_1 \vee C_2) =
\mathrm{int}(C_1) \cap \mathrm{int}(C_2)$, applied independently to each
component.

→ **`({h}, ∅)`** ✦ new

> Outer reduces to historical support only; inner rises to top because Linda Martin's
> inner concept is already the top — she has no inner-scale constraints.

**Nicole ⊔ Charlotte** → outer: `{p,h} ∩ {p,r,c}` = `{p}`, inner: `{a,m} ∩ {a}` = `{a}`
→ **`({p}, {a})`** ✦ new

> Both share political support; inner drops to bpm ≥ 100 only (minor constraint gone).

**Nicole ⊔ Šerifović** → outer: `{p,h} ∩ {c,h}` = `{h}`, inner: `{a,m} ∩ {a,b}` = `{a}`
→ **`({h}, {a})`** ✦ new

**Šerifović ⊔ Netta** → outer: `{c,h} ∩ {p,r}` = `∅`, inner: `{a,b} ∩ {a,m}` = `{a}`
→ **`(∅, {a})`** ✦ new

> No common outer attribute: the outer top concept appears.

**Charlotte ⊔ Šerifović** → outer: `{p,r,c} ∩ {c,h}` = `{c}`, inner: `{a} ∩ {a,b}` = `{a}`
→ **`({c}, {a})`** ✦ new

| `{p}`       | `{a}`        | Nicole ⊔ Charlotte      |
| `{h}`       | `{a}`        | Nicole ⊔ Šerifović      |
| `{p}`       | `{a, m}`     | Nicole ⊔ Netta          |
| `{r, c}`    | `∅`          | Linda Martin ⊔ Charlotte|
| `{c, h}`    | `∅`          | Linda Martin ⊔ Šerifović|
| `{r}`       | `∅`          | Linda Martin ⊔ Netta   |
| `{c}`       | `{a}`        | Charlotte ⊔ Šerifović   |
| `{p, c}`    | `{a}`        | Charlotte ⊔ Conchita    |
| `{p, r}`    | `{a}`        | Charlotte ⊔ Kalush      |
| `∅`         | `{a}`        | Šerifović ⊔ Netta       |

After Round 1: 9 original + 11 new = **20 pairs**.

### Round 2 and convergence

Joining the Round 1 new pairs with each other and with originals adds further pairs,
most importantly $(∅, ∅)$ — the outer top and inner top, corresponding to the top concept
of $\mathcal{B}(K)$.

The inner intents that can appear are constrained by the scale structure: because
bpm ≥ 150 implies bpm ≥ 100 in $K_\text{inner}$, no concept can have intent `{b}` alone.
The achievable inner intents in this subset are exactly `{a,b,m}`, `{a,b}`, `{a,m}`,
`{a}`, `∅` — five inner concepts.

> **Result:** Starting from 9 seed pairs, the join-closure algorithm terminates with
> **34 filled pairs**. These 34 pairs represent the complete subdirect product
> $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ — i.e., the 34 concepts of $\mathcal{B}(K)$ for this
> 9-winner subset.

### What 34 pairs means for the diagram

- At the outer **top** (no outer constraints), every inner concept is filled — all 5.
- At outer nodes with strong constraints (e.g., intent `{p,r,c,h}` = Nemo's only), only
  1 inner concept is filled: the bottom `{a,b,m}`.
- At intermediate outer nodes, the filled inner concepts form a consistent sub-pattern
  of the inner lattice.

Because $\varphi$ is injective, *each outer node has exactly as many filled inner nodes
as there are $\mathcal{B}(K)$ concepts projecting to that outer coordinate*. The nested
diagram is a lossless visual encoding of $\mathcal{B}(K)$.

## 7. Whiteboard Derivation

Here is how you would re-derive the algorithm from scratch, starting only from the goal.

### Step 1: name the goal

We want to compute, for each pair $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$, whether a concept
$C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$ exists with $\pi_\text{outer}(C) = c_\text{outer}$ and
$\pi_\text{inner}(C) = c_\text{inner}$. This is exactly $\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$.

### Step 2: notice that φ is a lattice homomorphism

$\varphi$ maps concepts to coordinate pairs and respects joins (Proposition 2). So
$\mathrm{image}(\varphi)$ is closed under joins — it is a sublattice. Any set
of generators under join-closure generates exactly that sublattice.

### Step 3: find the generators

By Proposition 1, every concept $C = \bigvee\{\gamma(g) : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}$.
So $\varphi(C) = \bigvee\{\varphi(\gamma(g)) : g \in \mathrm{ext}(C)\}$. The entire
image is generated by the *atomic pairs* $\{\varphi(\gamma(g)) : g \in G\}$ plus the
bottom pair.

### Step 4: replace B(K)-objects with factor-lattice objects

The atomic pairs require knowing $\gamma_K(g)$ inside $\mathcal{B}(K)$. The bridging lemma
(Part 4) resolves this: $\pi_\text{outer}(\gamma_K(g)) = \gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g)$. Both
factor object concepts are computable from the factor lattices alone.

### Step 5: the algorithm falls out

$$
\mathrm{image}(\varphi)
= \text{join-closure of }
\bigl\{(\gamma_{K_\text{outer}}(g),\; \gamma_{K_\text{inner}}(g)) : g \in G\bigr\}
\cup \{(\bot, \bot)\}
$$

"Join-closure in the product lattice" means: close under componentwise join in
$\mathcal{B}(K_\text{outer})$ and $\mathcal{B}(K_\text{inner})$ simultaneously. Both
joins are computable from the factor lattices.

> **Key insight:** The algorithm is not an ad hoc trick — it is the only algorithm that
> directly exploits the two structural properties of $\varphi$ (join-preservation) and
> $\mathcal{B}(K)$ (every concept equals the join of its object concepts). Remove either
> property and the algorithm breaks.

### Summary

Seed the set $P$ with one coordinate pair per object — the pair of factor-lattice object
concepts — plus the explicit bottom pair. Then repeatedly join any two pairs in $P$
componentwise and add the result to $P$ until no new pairs appear. This fixpoint is
exactly $\mathrm{image}(\varphi) = \mathcal{B}(K)$, encoded as pairs of concept
IDs from the two factor lattices. The nested diagram fills inner node $c_\text{inner}$
at outer node $c_\text{outer}$ iff the pair $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$ is in $P$.

## 8. Reading the Diagram: Filled Nodes, Hollow Nodes, and Implications

### What a filled node means

A filled node at position $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$ in the nested diagram means
exactly one thing: **there exists a concept $C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$ such that
$\varphi(C) = (c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$**. That concept $C$ is a genuine formal
concept of the combined context $K$ — a pair $(A, B)$ where $A$ is a closed set of
objects and $B = A'$ is their common attribute set. The nested diagram is a lossless
encoding: every filled node corresponds to exactly one concept in $\mathcal{B}(K)$, and
every concept in $\mathcal{B}(K)$ is represented by exactly one filled node.

### What a hollow node means

A hollow node at $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$ means the opposite: **no concept
$C \in \mathcal{B}(K)$ projects to this pair**. Equivalently, the combined attribute set
$\mathrm{int}(c_\text{outer}) \cup \mathrm{int}(c_\text{inner})$ is not a
closed set in $K$ — its closure strictly contains it.

Why would the closure be larger? Because there is an **implication** in $K$ that fires.
Specifically, there exists an implication $P \to Q$ (with $P \not\to Q$ being false in
$K$) such that:
$$
P \subseteq \mathrm{int}(c_\text{outer}) \cup \mathrm{int}(c_\text{inner})
\quad \text{but} \quad
Q \not\subseteq \mathrm{int}(c_\text{outer}) \cup \mathrm{int}(c_\text{inner})
$$

The premise $P$ is satisfied by the combined attributes of that node, but the conclusion
$Q$ introduces attributes not present — forcing any hypothetical object at this position
into a strictly more specific concept. The hollow node marks precisely this impossibility.

### Three types of implications, three types of hollow nodes

Because the attribute set of the combined context $K$ splits into outer and inner parts,
implications fall into three structural types, each producing a characteristic pattern of
hollow nodes:

**Inner → inner** ($P \subseteq M_\text{inner}$, $Q \subseteq M_\text{inner}$):
The implication connects musical attributes to each other. Its effect is visible within a
single outer bubble: certain inner nodes are hollow across *all* outer bubbles,
because the constraint holds universally regardless of the outer concept.

**Inner → outer** ($P \subseteq M_\text{inner}$, $Q \subseteq M_\text{outer}$):
A musical property forces a voting-pattern property. Nodes at the inner position of $P$
are hollow in outer bubbles whose intent does not include $Q$. To read this implication,
you must *compare across different outer bubbles* at the same inner level.

**Outer + inner → outer** ($P$ spans both scales, $Q \subseteq M_\text{outer}$):
A combination of voting and musical properties forces an additional voting property.
A node at position $(c_\text{outer}, c_\text{inner})$ is hollow when the outer intent of
$c_\text{outer}$ combined with the inner intent of $c_\text{inner}$ satisfies $P$, but
$c_\text{outer}$'s outer intent does not contain $Q$. The hollow node appears because any
actual object with those attributes would be carried into a different outer concept — one
that includes the forced outer attribute.

### The "look downward" heuristic and its limits

A useful heuristic for inner→inner implications: if an inner node $c_\text{inner}$ is
hollow at *every* outer bubble, scan the inner Hasse diagram downward from $c_\text{inner}$.
Any concept below it (with a larger inner intent) that is also universally hollow has an
intent whose closure exceeds it — evidence of an implication within $K_\text{inner}$.

However, this heuristic does not detect cross-scale implications. For inner→outer and
outer+inner→outer implications, hollow nodes appear *selectively* across outer bubbles,
not universally. Reading those implications requires comparing the fill pattern at a
fixed inner position across multiple outer bubbles.

### Implications and the Duquenne-Guigues basis

The minimal non-redundant set of implications of $K$ is the
**Duquenne-Guigues (stem) basis** [2]. Each implication in this basis is responsible
for one or more hollow nodes in the diagram. Conversely, every hollow node (except the
definitional bottom) can be traced to at least one basis implication whose premise is
satisfied by the node's combined attribute set.

The stem basis can be computed directly from the combined context $K$ using the
pseudo-intent algorithm. Because $K$ is never explicitly built in the ConceptFlow
implementation, a practical approach is to construct the combined incidence matrix from
the two factor contexts and run the pseudo-intent enumeration on that matrix, as
demonstrated in `examples/eurovision_nested_diagram.py`.
